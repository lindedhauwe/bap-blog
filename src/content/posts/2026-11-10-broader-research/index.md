## What did we do?

Until now, our research focused on our theme: children, books and reading. But an installation at a book fair is about more than that. Who can't join in? What does the installation do to the planet? Why do children like to play, and when does playing turn into frustration?

That's why we started extra research on Tuesday 10 November. Each of us took on a few themes and looked for sources: scientific articles, guidelines from museums and research by other organisations. For each source, we wrote down what it's about and what it found. The most important findings went on a **blue sticky note**, together with what it means for our concept.

## Playful learning

How do children learn through play, and what kind of play fits children aged 8 to 12?

**Key findings:**

- **Children aged 8 to 12 want to feel competent.** They like games with rules and a real challenge, played together with peers (Pubmed, 2023). So our installation needs a clear goal and rules, not aimless tinkering. It shouldn't feel childish, and it should be designed for children playing together, not only for a parent and a child.
- **Playful learning works best when it's joyful, meaningful, active, iterative and social** (Shaw, 2024). Surprise helps too: children learn more after something unexpected, like a twist or a cliffhanger. Let them try things out and see what happens, instead of looking for one right answer.
- **A simple, clear design works as well as or better than flashy graphics** (Clark et al., via the Joan Ganz Cooney Center). Schematic games did better than cartoonish or realistic ones. Flashy graphics can distract.
- **Moving and learning go well together and improve attention.** In a Dutch study with 499 children, physically active lessons led to 4 months of extra learning progress. We can build movement into the interaction: stepping, jumping, reaching, moving something. It also fits the energy of children at a fair. One nuance: this effect came after two years of repetition, so it says little about one visit.
- **A physical action only helps when it logically fits the content** (O'Malley & Fraser, 2004). Every button or action has to mean something in the story: opening a door, turning something over, putting two things together.
- **Physical objects make an installation more inviting** (Horn et al., 2009). In a museum, children were more actively involved with a tangible version than with a mouse, especially girls. Tangible objects also encourage working together, which helps several children play at the same time.
- **Let children act out the story** (the research behind the *Moved by Reading* method). Acting out what they hear or read, with objects or buttons, helps them understand and remember the story, and shows reading as something active.

## Inclusivity

> Inclusivity means that everyone can take part, belongs and is respected as an equal, regardless of background, identity or abilities.

We looked at four groups of children who could easily be left out.

### Dyslexia

Dyslexia is a learning disorder at word level: reading and spelling are slow and hard, despite effort. It affects about 3 to 10% of Dutch speakers and has nothing to do with intelligence.

**Key finding:** children with dyslexia do love stories, but drop out because reading takes so much effort. That's why our game gives clues mainly visually and out loud, in short pieces and together with a teammate, so everyone succeeds straight away and can experience the story without reading being a barrier.

What this means for our concept:

- Image before text: clues are visual, with colours, shapes and objects.
- Every text is also read aloud, ideally with highlighted words.
- No separate "dyslexia version" or special dyslexia font, because those aren't scientifically proven. We choose a clear sans-serif font with a lot of space, for everyone.
- Action and play are a strength, not a distraction. An Italian study found that children with dyslexia read faster and with fewer mistakes after playing an action game.
- Game first, book after. Just like "watch the film first", the child already knows the world after playing, which makes it easier to start a book in that genre.

### Inclusive experiences

We studied accessibility guidelines from museums: Perkins School for the Blind, the Smithsonian Guidelines for Accessible Exhibition Design, the Children's Museum & Theatre of Maine and Accessible Experiences. The most important lesson: *"There isn't simply a one size fits all."*

**Key finding:** make the game playable for everyone by giving every clue through image, sound *and* text, with large controllers you can use with one hand at a low height, in a safe, flat space without sudden flashes or loud noises.

What this means for our concept:

- Controllers: large (at least 7.5 cm), usable with one hand or a closed fist, and light.
- Height: important clues and interactions below about 90 cm, so children and wheelchair users can reach them.
- Every clue in image, text and sound, with subtitles. Short sentences and a high-contrast sans-serif font.
- No strobe lights or sudden flashes, and a volume button.
- At least 1.5 m of free space around the screen, a flat floor without cables, and a light strip if the space is dark.

### Movement with a disability

Researchers designed movement-based games together with young people in powered wheelchairs (Gerling et al., 2016). The games were engaging for players with very different abilities.

**Key finding:** movement games can also excite children with a physical disability, as long as every movement has an alternative, the difficulty adapts and they recognise themselves in the characters.

What this means for our concept:

- Jumping and ducking in the chase can also be done with an arm movement or a button on the controller.
- The flashlight and magnifying glass also work while seated, because you only aim them with your arm.
- Diverse characters, for example a suspect or an assistant in a wheelchair, without making it "the theme".
- Test with children with a disability, for example at a special needs school.

### Sensory sensitivity

Some children experience sound, light and crowds very intensely, for example children with autism, ADHD or high sensitivity.

**Key finding:** children who are sensitive to stimuli enjoy playing when it feels predictable and safe. So our game needs a clear structure with a fixed start and end, no sudden sounds or flashes, and a quiet mode or a calm place where they can recover.

What this means for our concept:

- Show beforehand how the game goes ("3 chapters: search → suspects → chase") and how long it still takes.
- A quiet mode with softer sound, less bright colours and no time pressure.
- The game ends at a calm moment, not in the middle of the action.
- Suggest a "quiet hour" to Boektopia, inspired by the Western Australian Museum, and a sensory info card at the stand: "It can be dark and loud here."

## More-than-human design and sustainability

Our installation is built for one fair, but its materials and electronics have an impact long after. So we compared materials and electronics on their pros and cons.

**Materials for the frame and the console dock:**

| Criterion | Honeycomb cardboard | EPS foam | Thermoformed plastic | Wood / plywood |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Weight of a 1200×800 panel | 2–3 kg | 1.5–2.5 kg | 2–4 kg | 10–15 kg |
| Sustained pressure | Very good | Moderate | Low over a large surface | Excellent |
| Shock absorption | Good | Excellent | Moderate | Low |
| Empty storage volume | Low (delivered flat) | Very high | High | Average |
| Tools | Few or none (digital) | Expensive mould | Expensive mould | Few |
| Moisture resistance | Limited | Excellent | Excellent | Good |
| End of life | Paper recycling | Difficult | Plastic recycling | Wood processing |

**Key findings:**

- **Honeycomb cardboard** is light, strong, recyclable, easy to replace, cheap and kid-proof, and there are fire-safe, PVC-free versions. It also fits the story of paper books. The downside: it's fragile and not moisture resistant. It's a common choice for fair stands and IKEA furniture.
- **Wood** is strong and can be moved flat, but it's heavy, needs more tools and can tip over without a solid fixing.
- **Screens and controllers** are only defensible if they are reused for years, edition after edition. LED screen tiles are reused for many events and can be rented. TVs can be rented or bought refurbished.
- **Wired (spiral) controllers** don't need charging, avoid Bluetooth problems and can't be stolen, as long as the cable is long enough to move around.
- **Book recommendations:** after a mini-game, we could also encourage families to borrow books from the library or buy second-hand, instead of only buying new books at Boektopia. Only recommending new books doesn't help the ecological footprint.

## Psychology

What makes a game fun, and when does it become frustrating?

**Key findings:**

- **Intuitive controls are key to feeling competent** (Ryan et al., 2006). Autonomy, competence and relatedness each predicted how much players enjoyed a game. So we use simple, clear buttons.
- **The biggest risk for frustration is children not understanding what to do** (Przybylski et al., 2014). Frustration came from not mastering the controls or the difficulty, not from the story or the visuals. Children are even less able to regulate frustration than adults (Bunge et al., 2002). On a busy fair, this is extra important.
- **Curiosity works like a reward** (Gruber et al., 2014). People remember information better when they're curious, and curiosity also improves memory in children aged 10 to 14 (Fandakova & Gruber, 2021).
- **Children have to decide together** (Tenenbaum et al., 2019). Learning with peers works as well as one-on-one with an adult, but only saying "work together" doesn't help. So we design the interaction so a duo has to make a decision together, for example pressing a button at the same time or choosing together where the story goes. Two children playing side by side isn't enough.
- **Four kinds of fun** (Lazzaro, 2004). A good installation usually combines several, for example discovering something (Easy Fun) and beating a challenge together (People Fun and Hard Fun).

| Game type | Feeling | Type of fun | Risk |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Finding or discovering something | Curiosity, wonder, surprise | Easy Fun | Can become too non-committal without a goal |
| Beating or overcoming something | Triumph ("fiero"), competence | Hard Fun | Frustration if it's too hard or unclear |
| Something calm | Relaxation, concentration | Serious Fun | Little research found on children |
| Together (duo) | Fun, connectedness | People Fun | Only playing side by side isn't enough, they have to decide together |

## Needs and values of our target audience

We brought together everything we learned so far into the needs and values of our target audience, split into children and parents. Every point is backed by our [online survey](../2026-10-29-survey-results/), our [interviews at Boektopia](../2026-11-03-boektopia-visit-interviews/), our observations or our desk research.

| | Children (6–12) | Parents |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Needs** | Moving and doing things themselves · Excitement and adventure: searching, discovering, beating something · Humour · Playing together, with or against each other · Safety: not alone in public · Quick results, an "I can do it" feeling · Something tangible to take home · Choosing and having influence · A short experience | Doing something together with the child · Choosing between joining in and watching · A short waiting time · A short experience · Low threshold: no reservation, immediately clear · Being surprised by something unexpected or funny · A memory or personal result · Compact, fits in a busy space |
| **Values** | Fun · Pride and ownership · Curiosity · Connectedness with family and siblings · Independence (from about 6 years old) | Family time and tradition · The child comes first · Reading for fun without pressure · Their own time and interests · Discovering and finding new books |

## Impact after the event

What should our installation leave behind once the fair is over?

| | Short term | Long term |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Child** | Had fun, discovered a genre, chose a book themselves | Knows which genre suits them, reads more widely and reads because they want to. Reading in that genre feels fun and self-chosen, not like homework. |
| **Parent** | Played together with their child, got concrete book advice | Has a starting point to read and talk together, and can build on the child's interests. |
| **Boektopia** | More book sales per genre, a lively stand, enthusiastic children | Families who come back for a new genre. Photos and stories that are passed on bring more visitors to future editions. |

## What did we learn?

By looking beyond books, we found a lot of things we hadn't thought about before: a child in a wheelchair who can't jump, a child with dyslexia who loves stories but not reading, a child who gets overwhelmed by flashing lights, and screens that are thrown away after one fair. Many of these solutions help everyone, not only one group: clues in image, sound and text, large and simple controls, a clear structure and playing together.

## Next steps

- Choose our materials and electronics, with honeycomb cardboard as our first option.
- Test with a diverse group of children, ideally also at a special needs school.
