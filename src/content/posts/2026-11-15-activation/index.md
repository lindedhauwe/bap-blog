## What did we do?

On 15 and 16 November, we worked on the activation phase: translating our concept into the concrete experience of a visitor. We wanted to know how a family discovers, uses and leaves our installation, and what we need to design so everyone can get started easily. We used four methods: a **user journey**, a **storyboard**, **instruction guidelines** and **levels of expertise**.

## 1. User journey

We mapped the full journey of a family, from walking around Boektopia to telling others about the experience. We split that journey into six phases: awareness, interest, desire, action, retention and loyalty. For each step, we wrote down what the visitor does, feels and thinks, which touchpoints they come across, and what the pain points and opportunities are.

**What we learned:**

- **The experience starts before the game.** The stand has to stand out in a busy hall, and visitors have to understand quickly what's going on. That's why an eye-catching, decorated stand and a big screen you can see from all sides are important.
- **Waiting is a critical moment.** Young children find waiting boring, and long queues put parents off. So we keep the games short and make sure people waiting can see the screen and already learn by watching.
- **During the game, it's all about clarity.** The biggest pain points are an unclear start, not knowing who has to do what, and a game that's too hard or too easy. Visual explanations, a voice-over and clear roles are the solution.
- **The end always has to feel positive.** Players should never feel like they "lost". Everyone reaches the goal, and a clear end screen closes the game.
- **After the game, the experience could get lost.** Visitors might not notice the card, not know what to do with it, or not find the stamps. So the card has to be visually attractive and clearly explain what to do, and the stamp stands have to stand out.

## 2. Storyboard

In a storyboard of ten scenes, we followed Lotte (10) and her dad Tom during their visit to Boektopia. We drew how they notice the stand, watch, join the queue, play the fantasy game together, take their card and finally discover a book in the fantasy section.

**What we learned:**

- **The storyboard made the experience tangible.** We no longer saw the concept as separate parts, but as one story with a beginning, a middle and an end.
- **The emotional moments became visible:** the curiosity when noticing the stand, the discussion about the genre, the high five after the game and the pride when getting a stamp. Those are exactly the moments we want to strengthen in our design.
- **The story doesn't stop at the screen.** The last scenes show that the real goal is only reached when Lotte finds a book and immediately wants to discover the next genre.

## 3. Instruction guidelines

We wrote out the steps a user goes through, from discovering the installation to keeping the card as a souvenir. For each step, we added emojis to show how we expect the visitor to feel.

**What we learned:**

- **Every step has to be short and unambiguous.** We deliberately kept the instructions simple: one action per step, in easy language.
- **The emotions show that the experience builds up:** from curiosity and doubt at the start, to focus and fun during the game, to pride and enthusiasm at the end.
- **The steps after the game are just as important as the game itself:** putting the controllers back, taking the card, looking for the books and getting the stamp. They keep the flow smooth and create the link with real books.

## 4. Levels of expertise

We divided our users into four levels: beginner, intermediate, advanced and expert. For each level, we described who the user is (child and parent), what their characteristics are, what they need and what that means for our design.

| Level | Who | What they need |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Beginner** | Children of 8 to 9 who don't game or read much, and parents who feel unsure about technology. They hesitate to join in and give up quickly. | Instructions with little text, direct feedback, the certainty that they always win, and the reassurance that it doesn't take long. They prefer to learn by watching first while they're in the queue. |
| **Intermediate** | Players who understand the game quickly after watching once and recognise some of the characters. | The feeling of winning, but not too easily. Difficulty that grows during the game, and clear roles. |
| **Advanced** | Players who quickly find the game too easy or childish. | More challenge, a visual style that isn't too childish, and something to collect, like the stamp card. |
| **Expert** | Children who already read a lot and know many genres. | Less well-known book recommendations, and encouragement to try new genres. |

**Key insight:** we don't design for one type of user. Our game has to be simple enough for beginners and challenging enough for advanced players at the same time. Growing difficulty within each game, recognisable book references at different levels (from popular to less well-known) and the stamp card as an extra goal help us reach every level.

## 🎯 Conclusion

Thanks to the activation phase, we understand better how a family really experiences our installation, from the first glance at the stand to the moment they take a book home.

The most important lessons:

- **Clarity comes first.** Visitors have to know what to do without explanation, thanks to visual instructions, a voice-over, clear roles and a clear start and end.
- **Every player has to succeed.** Nobody loses, but the game has to stay challenging enough for older or experienced players.
- **Waiting is part of the experience.** By watching, new players learn how it works, and short games keep the queue short.
- **The experience has to continue after the game.** The card, the stamps and the book recommendations are the bridge to real books, so they have to be designed as carefully as the game itself.

## Next steps

We take these insights to the next phase, where we'll work out the screens, the props and the card in detail and test them with our target group.
